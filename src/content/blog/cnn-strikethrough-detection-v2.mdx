## The problem

Here is a line from a real patent amendment:

<figure>
  <Image src={realLine} alt="A scanned line reading 'Amended) The An assay test', with 'The' underlined and 'An' struck through" width={600} />
  <figcaption>The applicant deleted "An" and inserted "The".</figcaption>
</figure>

Here is what OCR gives you for it:

```text
Amended) The An assay test
```

Modern OCR providers often do not provide text style annotations. For the vast
majority of documents, this is fine. The meaning is preserved. For patent
amendments, it isn't. Underline marks inserted text and strikethrough marks
deleted text, so they are key to an LLM understanding the document. Without
them, "The An assay test" is just a typo. Even worse, OCR providers often drop
stricken words altogether.

<MotionOcrLoss />

At Solve, our existing solution required the agent to view the pdf using image
tokens. This approach has a few key disadvantages:

- **Context management**: A page rendered at 200 DPI costs roughly 4,800 tokens
  as an image. The same page as text is closer to 750, so viewing it costs about
  six times more.
- **Accuracy**: Surprisingly, modern language models struggle with this task,
  perhaps just due to a lack of training on it.
- **Grepability**: Over a large corpus of documents, searching for specific
  amendments is much more difficult, as it requires viewing each document in
  turn.

These drawbacks forced us to only support `.DOCX` uploads for various features
that rely on amendments, which can be frustrating to users as the vast majority
of the platform supports PDF uploads.

## A tiny model for strikethrough and underline

Motivated by the above, I challenged myself to come up with a model that could
detect strikethrough and underline on OCR'ed documents. The solution had a few
key requirements:

- **Accuracy**: $>98\%$ of words exactly right was the target.
- **Inference cost**: Must be able to run on our existing document ingestion
  pipeline workers, without significantly slowing down the pipeline.

This fits exactly the requirements of the tiny models I am most interested in
building (see my [master's project](/blog/final-year-project/)), so I was
immediately [nerd sniped](https://xkcd.com/356/).

This is the solution we landed on. 227,746 parameters and 834 KB. Small enough
to run in your browser, accurate enough to be used in production, trained on a
MacBook.

<LiveStrikethrough />

The inputs to the model are:

1. A grayscale image of size $W \times 64$, cropped around one OCR text box.
2. The OCR text for that box, up to 160 codepoints, which the model assumes is
   correct. Each codepoint $c$ becomes its four UTF-8 bytes and a hash into 2,047
   buckets:

$$
h(c) = 1 + (c \cdot 2654435761) \bmod 2047
$$

The output is two independent probabilities per codepoint, strikethrough and
underline, each thresholded at $0.5$:

$$
p \in [0, 1]^{C \times 2}
$$

## Development

I was first introduced to the solution space of this problem when Robin
implemented a classical computer vision pipeline to detect lines through text.
This validated that the idea was possible, and gave him a great intuition for
the problem.

My first attempt was a simple CNN classifier that operated on the image only,
classifying each vertical slice, providing further validation that this could be
solved with a small model. However, there was no way to map the extracted styles
back on to the OCR text.

### Agentic development

Increasingly, software is being written in such a way that the code itself does
not matter, only its contract with the outside world, performance, and
correctness. This kind of model development is an excellent example of a problem
that benefits from this, as it has very clear success metrics.

So, I posed the issue to Claude Opus 5.5 and left it. The solution it came up
with was one of the more interesting model architectures I have ever seen. The
image goes through a tiny ConvNeXt into a shared memory, and then every character
in the OCR text asks that memory a question: where is my ink, and is there a line
through it?

That mapping problem from my first attempt just disappears. Characters don't need
aligning to columns, because each one finds its own.

### Starting from morphology

Robin's classical pipeline was the proof that this could work at all. It also showed
why it needed learning. Morphology cannot tell what a horizontal run is for: table
rules, chemical bonds and fraction bars all look like a strike.

### Trying to delete the aligner

LineNet worked, but it needed CTC forced alignment to get from columns back to the
OCR text, and alignment has no good answer when the text and the ink disagree. So I
tried to get rid of it. Each character would find its own ink, using cross-attention
and pointers.

That is the idea that eventually shipped, but it did not work the first time.
Pointer decoding scored 0.66 on marked lines, against 0.87 for CTC on the same
network. So alignment came back, and the release of the model built to remove
alignment decoded its styles with alignment.

### Too ambitious

The other thing I wanted was to recover the struck text that OCR had dropped, with a
gap head and a separate reader. The reader was great. Finding the gaps was not. On
simulated deletions, it located 85% of dropped runs. On genuine OCR drops, it located
10–50%, on just 42 and 8 runs. They are rare, so there is very little to learn from.

The gains also weren't where I expected. On clean USPTO scans, v10 barely moved
against the previous release: 0.886 against 0.889. The real gain was on EPO, 0.824
to 0.895. Meanwhile one of the biggest jumps in false positives came from changing no
architecture at all: hard negatives, a rule filter and a new operating point took
LineNet from 2.10 false words per 10 pages to 0.26.

None of this line was ever merged.

### Narrowing the problem

Robin's version asked a smaller question. Assume the text is correct, and predict
only strikethrough and underline for each codepoint. No CTC, no text recovery, no
distillation, no postprocessing. When the text looks wrong, the AI is told to look
at the PDF.

Dropping CTC was not a free win. On my tagger, turning the aligner off with the same
weights lost 5.5 to 19 points on the dev sets. It took a different task and a
different model to make it work.

That is the model above. It was pretrained on 20,000 steps of synthetic lines in
seven languages, fine-tuned on real crops, selected at step 1,800, and then
adapted with the backbone frozen. One identity-initialized attention block went in,
12 tensors changed, and exactness rose by about 0.39 percentage points at almost
the same latency.

Not everything helped. A later run regressed and was rejected, as was a later
synthetic generator. One of the biggest limits was not the model at all: whole
styled lines had been labelled plain in the gold data.

### The trouble with 4s

EPO amendments are the hardest thing this model sees, because claims get renumbered
one digit at a time:

<figure>
  <Image src={renumberedLine} alt="A scanned EPO claim line reading 814. The apparatus of any of claims 58 to 713, with single digits underlined and struck through" />
</figure>

Within that, the digit 4 is its own problem, because its crossbar sits right where a
strike would go. To measure this, I rendered plain digits between struck neighbours,
with the strike drawn at crossbar height. The model called a plain 4 struck 18 times
out of 60. It did that 0 times out of 60 for each of 1, 7 and 8. And the illusion
comes from the neighbouring ink: blank out both neighbours, and the mean probability
of strike on the 4 falls from 0.326 to 0.001.

It goes wrong the other way too, and on real documents this is the bigger problem. In
digit runs with both struck and unstruck digits, all of them from EPO, the model
misses 8 of 12 struck 4s, against 41 of 160 for other digits. It misses 8 of 9 struck
4s where neither neighbour is struck, so the context is pointing the wrong way.

The attention is bidirectional. The self-attention block between the two
cross-attention blocks is unmasked, so each character can see both its left and its
right. That sounds like the fix for a 4 between struck neighbours, but I can't show
that it is. Without the block, the model already got all 34 of those right, and a
plain 4 between struck neighbours never appears in the dev set. Across 399 scored 4s,
adding the block fixed none and broke one. Limiting it to one side moved at most 3 of
them.

What the block does help is everything around them. It fixed 21 errors and broke 5 on
other digits, though that holds with the attention update zeroed, so I can't credit
the attention itself. The attention does help underline: 17 lines fixed and 5 broken,
against the block with attention zeroed. Direction matters there too. Losing the right
side costs 30 lines and gains back 5, mostly on underline, while losing the left has no
detectable cost.

So 4s remain partly unsolved. With only about a dozen errors to work with, these
numbers can't rule out a small effect either way.

## Taking it apart

Thirteen generations leaves you with a lot of parts and no idea which ones earned
their place. So I took the model apart and retrained it, removing one thing at a
time: 76 runs, about six hours on CPU.

Retraining is the important word. My first attempt just switched parts off in the
trained model, and that massively overstates things, because the remaining parts
have learned to depend on them. Switching off image position cost 94 percentage
points. Removing it and letting the model adapt cost 3.7. The second cross-attention
block's attention went from 22.7 points to 0.2.

The noise is the other thing to get right. I included a placebo that changed one
training character out of the whole set, and it moved accuracy by 0.17 points and
passed the significance tests. So I ignore anything under about a quarter of a point,
whatever the p-value says. Training the whole thing from scratch three times gave
96.22%, 96.68% and 97.14%, so one seed can't tell you much.

The "at most" rows are inputs I removed from an already pretrained model before fine
tuning it, which is why they are limits and not measurements. The biggest ones also
reset some weights, so their order isn't reliable.

The biggest surprise for me is that the top result has nothing to do with the network.
Training on whole lines is worth more than any input. Without it, line accuracy falls
to 85%. And it works both ways: training only on lines might gain 0.45 points on
lines, but loses 7.97 on words. Each view mostly serves itself.

Position is the most useful thing the model gets, especially for digits. Errors in
mixed digit runs nearly triple without relative position. Human
review of the training labels is also worth a real 1.28 points, although half of that
loss comes from one document.

That leaves the 4s, and they don't get any easier. None of the things I removed turned
out to be specifically about 4s: everything that hurt them also hurt other digits.
Retraining with causal self-attention, so each character can only see to its left,
cost 0.19 points overall, not enough to be sure of, and changed the error rate on 4s
by exactly nothing. That is a smaller cost than the masks above suggested, which is
the same lesson again.

The hash embedding and the second cross-attention block are about 14% of the
parameters each, and neither loss could be detected. They might be removable. I
wouldn't remove them yet, because the second block had only one seed, and nobody has
tried removing both.

One result is bigger than all of these. On ten documents held out of training, lines
exactly right fell to 84–88%, against about 97% on the dev set. I can't yet tell
whether those documents are harder or their labels are noisier. But it does make me
think that the next point is more likely to come from data than from architecture.

## Running it

In production, it runs on CPU with ONNX Runtime, in one worker process per parent:
two threads, batches of 8, and at most two pages rendered at 200 DPI. Inference
failures never fail OCR.

In your browser, it also runs on CPU. It does run on WebGPU, but it returns
different numbers, so I stopped trusting it. The runtime is 14.8 MB, which is about
18 times the size of the model.
