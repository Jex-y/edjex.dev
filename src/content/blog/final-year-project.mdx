## The constraint

<Row>

Project Robocoenosis builds underwater devices that use living organisms as sensors. They run on microbial fuel cells, so every millijoule decides how often they can look.

Water fleas spin when something in the water stresses them, which makes them a cheap detector for pollutants nobody thought to test for.<NoteRef n={1} /> Watching them needs computer vision on the device itself, because radio does not get out of the water and storing video is not real time. The model I ended up with is small enough that [it runs on this page](#run-it-here), on your own GPU.

<Note slot="left" n={1}>*Daphnia*, a few millimetres long. Healthy ones swim in short hops; stressed ones go round in circles.</Note>

## Points, not boxes

<Row>

A standard detector predicts boxes and classes at several scales, through tens of layers. Water fleas are all about the same size, and only where they are and how they move matters. So the model predicts one heatmap peak per animal, in the style of CenterNet,<NoteRef n={2} /> and everything else a detector carries is thrown away.

<BoxesVsPoints>Left, what a standard detector predicts; right, what this one predicts: one point per animal and a small offset.</BoxesVsPoints>

<Note slot="right" n={2}>Zhou, Wang and Krähenbühl, *Objects as Points*, 2019.</Note>

</Row>

## Search, then measure

<Row>

I sampled five hundred architectures at random, varying depth, input resolution, width and block type, and trained them across a GPU cluster. Each was quantised to eight bits and compiled for the accelerator.<NoteRef n={3} />

Then every one of them ran on the bench. Estimating energy from the architecture works on a CPU but not on an accelerator, so there was no shortcut past measuring.<NoteRef n={4} />

<Note slot="left" n={3}>Compiled with the Hailo toolchain for a Hailo-8L on a Raspberry Pi 5.</Note>

<Note slot="right" n={4}>On the CPU, architectural features explain 80% of the variance in energy. On the accelerator, 15%.</Note>

</Row>

<WideFigure src={bench} alt="A precision power supply wired to a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8L accelerator">A Keithley 2280S fed the Raspberry Pi and sampled its current at 100 Hz, triggered over GPIO, with Wi-Fi off and a cool-down between runs.</WideFigure>

## Two convolutions and a head

<Row>

The best model on the Pareto front, KEYNAS5-204, is three layers deep: two convolutions and a detection head. It reaches an average precision of 0.948 at 2.2 µJ a frame, and runs at 5,167 frames a second.

<figure>

| Model       | Energy per frame | Frames per second |
| :---------- | ---------------: | ----------------: |
| YOLO11-nano |         1,449 µJ |                73 |
| KEYNAS5-204 |           2.2 µJ |             5,167 |

<figcaption>Measured on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-8L.</figcaption>

</figure>

A few things surprised me. Every model on the Pareto front has five layers or fewer.<NoteRef n={5} /> On the accelerator, speed and energy are almost the same thing.<NoteRef n={6} /> And dropping to four bits saved nothing measurable over eight.

## Run it here

<Row>

A model this small doesn't need an accelerator to be fast. Below, KEYNAS5-204 runs in your browser on frames from the held-out test set, which it never saw in training. Every square, dot and heatmap is computed live, on your machine, as the page shows it.

<LiveDetector>Left, each water flea the model finds: the square is the heatmap cell that fired, and the dot is where the offset moves it. Right, the heatmap itself, at half the resolution the model sees.</LiveDetector>

The whole network is 12,387 numbers, about 50 KB, smaller than most of the photos on this page.<NoteRef n={7} /> There is no inference library. Each layer is one WebGPU compute shader: a 3×3 convolution in which every GPU thread works out a single output value. The three layers run back to back, and the result comes back to the page as three 96×96 grids: the heatmap and the two offsets.

Decoding is the same as on the device. A sigmoid turns the first grid into scores, a 3×3 max filter keeps only the local peaks, anything under 0.5 is dropped, and each survivor moves by its offset from the centre of its cell to the animal.

A frame is about 114 million multiply-adds, which a laptop GPU gets through in a few milliseconds. Without WebGPU, the same arithmetic runs as plain JavaScript in around a hundred.<NoteRef n={8} />

<Note slot="right" n={7}>Exported from the same trained run that was compiled for the Hailo-8L.</Note>

<Note slot="right" n={8}>The shader and the decoding are in [`src/scripts/detector`](https://github.com/Jex-y/edjex.dev/tree/main/src/scripts/detector) in this site's repository.</Note>

</Row>
